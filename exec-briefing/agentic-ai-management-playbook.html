From Tool to Coworker:
How Agentic AI Rewrites the Management Playbook

AI agents are no longer experimental. They plan, act, and learn on their own. The management frameworks your organization runs on were not designed for this — and the gap is already costing leaders real value.

Arnay Joshi · March 2026 · ⏱ ~90 seconds · ← All Executive Briefings
The Insight

In a landmark 2025 global study of 2,102 senior leaders across 21 industries and 116 countries, MIT Sloan Management Review and Boston Consulting Group surfaced a number that should stop every executive in their tracks: 76% of respondents now view agentic AI as more like a coworker than a tool. That is not a metaphor. It is a structural reality that invalidates how most organizations currently govern, invest in, and measure AI.

The MIT Sloan / BCG research identified four core tensions that no existing management framework adequately addresses: scalability versus adaptability (constrain agents too much and they lose value; give them too much freedom and you introduce unpredictability); experience versus expediency (agentic AI simultaneously depreciates through model drift and appreciates through continuous learning, breaking traditional financial models); supervision versus autonomy (excessive oversight negates the productivity gain, while insufficient oversight creates liability); and retrofit versus reengineer (a tactical patch to existing workflows versus a deliberate redesign from the ground up).

The implications are compounding rapidly. A BCG 2025 study found that only 5% of companies have achieved AI value at scale, with 60% reporting no material returns despite substantial investment. McKinsey’s State of AI research confirms a similar picture: nearly nine in ten organizations use AI regularly, but only about one in three has scaled meaningfully beyond pilots. The bottleneck is not the technology. A Gartner survey found that only 13% of IT leaders believe their governance structures are adequate to manage autonomous agents. The frameworks have not kept pace.

What This Means for You
  • Retire the tool-versus-worker binary. Agentic AI demands a hybrid management model — one that applies human-resource-style governance (accountability, role definition, performance review) alongside asset-management discipline (versioning, drift monitoring, investment timing). Organizations still treating agents as software deployments are mismanaging them.
  • Establish a governance board before you scale. MIT’s Kate Kellogg and her research team are direct: as you move agency from humans to machines, the importance of governance infrastructure rises in direct proportion. A cross-functional AI governance board — with specific individuals owning monitoring, safety enforcement, and accountability — is not a compliance exercise. It is a prerequisite for value.
  • Redefine leadership roles around policy, not planning. When agentic systems can reconfigure workflows autonomously, architects become policy stewards and developers become validators. Senior leaders who wait for these role shifts to happen organically will find themselves behind. Redesign accountability structures now, before agents make the decision for you.
Practitioner’s Take

The organizations that will win with agentic AI are not the ones that deployed the most agents the fastest. They are the ones that figured out governance before scale — who owns the agent when it fails, who signs off on its scope, and what the rollback plan looks like. Autonomy without accountability is not transformation. It is technical debt with a headcount problem attached to it.
